Nicotine withdrawal: what’s normal and how long it lasts

Withdrawal means your body is adjusting, and it is temporary. Here is what you may feel, how long it usually lasts and what helps.

  1. Symptoms usually peak in the first 3 days. You’re in the hardest part, and it’s short.
  2. You’re past the peak. Symptoms ease noticeably within 2–4 weeks.
  3. Most symptoms are behind you. Appetite or the odd craving can linger a while, and that’s normal.

Headaches

How long does it last?
Usually 1–2 weeks
Why does it happen?
Nicotine narrowed your blood vessels. Blood flow rebalances as your body adjusts.
What helps?
  • Drink plenty of water through the day.
  • Don’t skip meals; keep your blood sugar steady.
  • A short walk or fresh air helps.
When to see a doctor
If the pain is severe or lasts longer than 2 weeks.

Irritability, tension

How long does it last?
Peaks in the first week, eases in 2–4 weeks
Why does it happen?
Your brain is getting used to working without nicotine. It’s a temporary adjustment.
What helps?
  • Cut back on coffee: smoking cleared caffeine fast, so the same cup now hits harder.
  • Try the breathing exercise on the SOS screen.
  • Tell the people around you that you’re quitting.
When to see a doctor
If tension makes daily life hard to manage.

Trouble sleeping

How long does it last?
Usually 1–2 weeks
Why does it happen?
Your sleep was tuned to nicotine. Vivid dreams and waking at night are common for a while.
What helps?
  • No caffeine after midday.
  • Spend the last hour before bed screen-free.
  • Move during the day so you’re tired in the evening.
When to see a doctor
If poor sleep lasts longer than 3–4 weeks.

Bigger appetite

How long does it last?
A few weeks, sometimes a few months
Why does it happen?
Nicotine suppressed your appetite and your sense of taste is recovering. Your hands and mouth also miss the habit.
What helps?
  • Keep crunchy snacks like carrots or cucumber ready.
  • Chew sugar-free gum.
  • Eat regular meals and drink water.
When to see a doctor
If weight gain worries you, a dietitian can help. A few kilos are tiny next to the health benefits of quitting.

Trouble concentrating

How long does it last?
Usually 1–2 weeks
Why does it happen?
Your brain is relearning to work without the stimulation nicotine gave it.
What helps?
  • Break tasks into small pieces.
  • Take short breaks and walk.
  • Try not to plan heavy work for the first days.
When to see a doctor
If there’s still a clear problem after a month.

Coughing

How long does it last?
Can increase for a few weeks, then fades
Why does it happen?
A good sign: the tiny hairs in your lungs are working again and clearing out built-up mucus.
What helps?
  • Drink plenty of water.
  • Warm drinks and steam soothe.
  • Light exercise speeds up the clearing.
When to see a doctor
If you cough up blood, have a fever or feel short of breath, or the cough lasts over 4 weeks.

Low mood, sadness

How long does it last?
Usually 2–4 weeks
Why does it happen?
Smoking triggered pleasure and relief artificially. Your brain is relearning to do that on its own.
What helps?
  • Go for a walk in daylight.
  • Talk to a friend; don’t stay alone with it.
  • Celebrate small wins: look at your badges.
When to see a doctor
If sadness lasts over 2 weeks or you’ve had depression before, talk to a professional. If you have thoughts of harming yourself, call your local emergency number now.

Dizziness

How long does it last?
Usually the first few days
Why does it happen?
There’s more oxygen in your blood and your body is adjusting to it.
What helps?
  • Sit or lie down slowly.
  • Breathe slowly and deeply.
  • Avoid sudden movements.
When to see a doctor
If it lasts more than a few days or you feel faint.

Constipation

How long does it last?
The first few weeks
Why does it happen?
Nicotine stimulated your gut. Your digestion is adjusting to the new routine.
What helps?
  • Eat fibre: vegetables, fruit, whole grains.
  • Drink plenty of water.
  • Walk a little every day.
When to see a doctor
If you have severe stomach pain or it lasts over 2 weeks.

Sources: NHS, CDC and the US National Cancer Institute. This is general information, not medical advice.
